Abstract

Driver fatigue remains a leading contributor to Kenya’s alarming road traffic fatality rate, with studies attributing nearly one in five serious accidents to drowsy driving. Despite public awareness campaigns and intermittent enforcement at rest stops, private motorists lack affordable, real-time tools to monitor their alertness. This gap leaves countless drivers relying on unreliable strategies, such as caffeine boosts or loud music, that often prove insufficient on lengthy or nocturnal journeys.

Without intervention, micro-sleep episodes and brief lapses in consciousness lasting mere seconds can turn routine drives into life-threatening crashes. Existing commercial fatigue detection systems are either prohibitively expensive or poorly suited to secondhand vehicles, while smartphone apps depend on user calibration and fail under low-light or noisy conditions. The absence of an integrated alert mechanism compounds the risk for everyday drivers in both urban and rural settings.

Vigi-Drive bridges this divide by combining Python-based facial landmark analysis (via OpenCV and Mediapipe/Dlib) with an Arduino-controlled buzzer to deliver a tactile alert within 0.5 seconds of detecting extended eye closure or yawning. The development will proceed through agile, two-week sprints that incrementally refine detection thresholds and hardware integration. Controlled laboratory trials under varied illumination will validate at least 90% accuracy in fatigue classification and 95% reliability in alarm activation.

By integrating unobtrusive vision-based monitoring with an unmistakable audible warning, Vigi-Drive delivers a low-cost, replicable solution tailored to the Kenyan driving context. Its modular architecture and comprehensive documentation facilitate easy reproduction and future enhancements, paving the way for broader deployment and, ultimately, a reduction in fatigue-related road fatalities.

Chapter One: Introduction

Background
Road traffic crashes pose a serious threat to public safety across Kenya. The National Transport and Safety Authority (NTSA) reports that 1,139 people died in road accidents between January and March 2025, averaging nearly twelve fatalities per day nationally (The Star, 2025). In the first half of 2024, 2,407 Kenyans lost their lives in crashes, alongside over 11,502 serious injuries (Africa, 2024). These figures reflect a persistently high fatality rate estimated at 28 deaths per 100,000 population, among the highest in sub-Saharan Africa (The Kenyan Diaspora Media, 2024).

Casualties span all road users, but private vehicles feature prominently among fatal crashes. NTSA data indicate that 342 deadly accidents involving privately owned cars occurred in early 2024, outnumbering those linked to public-service vehicles, such as matatus and buses. Such incidents occur on highways and rural roads alike, from the busy Nairobi–Mombasa corridor to county roads in Machakos and Kitui. For example, on 31 January 2025, two occupants died when their car plunged into the Thwake River in Machakos; police suggested the driver “might have been asleep” given the lack of skid marks (The Star, 2025).

Human error remains at the heart of Kenya’s crash epidemic. While speeding and drunk driving are well-known risks, driver drowsiness has emerged as a leading behavioural cause. In a joint press statement, Transport and Interior ministry officials listed fatigue alongside speeding and drunk driving as primary triggers for fatal collisions. NTSA’s analyses confirm that loss of control by a fatigued driver is repeatedly cited in crash investigations (Maureen Kinyanjui, 2024).

Long-distance and night-time driving amplify the danger. Motorists commuting after long workdays or undertaking overnight holiday trips often experience slowed reactions and “micro-sleep” episodes, brief lapses in awareness lasting only seconds that can have deadly consequences. In December 2023, for instance, over 466 people died in road crashes, making it the deadliest month of the year (Kinyanjui, 2025).
Despite these grim trends, most Kenyan drivers have no in-vehicle assistance to alert them when fatigue sets in. Factory-fitted driver-alert systems are typically found only in high-end imports, while aftermarket fleet-tracking solutions are priced for commercial operators. Ordinary motorists driving second-hand saloons, hatchbacks, or small SUVs rely on self-monitoring methods such as taking coffee breaks or listening to loud music, which offer only fleeting relief.

This nationwide toll of deaths and injuries, along with the proven role of drowsiness in many crashes, highlights the urgent need for affordable interventions that help drivers stay alert on every Kenyan road.

Justification(s)
When setting out to address Kenya’s alarming road fatality rates, it became clear that any effective solution must go beyond theory. Driver drowsiness is a subtle but deadly problem: a momentary lapse in attention can turn a routine drive into a tragedy. After reviewing existing research and consulting commuters who travel long distances, it was determined that a real-time warning system, one that actively watches for the tell-tale signs of fatigue and responds immediately, would fill the gap where “self-monitoring” strategies have failed.

VigiDrive is built around facial landmark analysis because it directly targets the body’s natural signals of sleepiness, such as slow blinking and yawning, rather than relying on indirect measures like steering patterns or seat-pressure sensors. By focusing on the driver’s face, the system can detect drowsiness at its earliest stage. To keep development on track, an Agile approach is adopted: each two-week sprint adds new features to reduce detection thresholds based on hands-on testing. This iterative method enables a rapid response to real-world feedback, adjusting sensitivity, reducing false alarm rates and adapting to various lighting conditions in Kenyan traffic.

Equally important is the seamless integration of software and hardware. When the facial-analysis module spots fatigue, it does more than log the event or flash a message on screen; it triggers an Arduino-driven buzzer that the driver cannot ignore. This physical alert bridges the gap between digital detection and human response, increasing the likelihood that a dozing driver will regain alertness before a micro-sleep turns into a life-threatening error.

VigiDrive’s design and documentation support both academic evaluation and future extension. The system is structured around clear user stories, detects fatigue, sends alerts, and logs events, each backed by testable acceptance criteria. The modular architecture enables the replacement of alternative algorithms or different alert devices without requiring a rework of the entire codebase. 

Scope and Limitations
1.5.1 Scope of the Project
The scope of Vigi-Drive covers the development of a complete, working prototype within one academic year. During this project, a Python application will be developed to capture live video from a standard webcam, apply facial-landmark analysis to detect prolonged eye closure and yawning, and flag drowsiness events in real time. When fatigue is detected, the software sends a serial signal to an Arduino board, which activates a buzzer to alert the user.

A basic logging mechanism will record the timestamp and measured facial landmark values for each event, allowing for later review. Clear documentation, including code samples, wiring diagrams, and test results, will be prepared so that another student can reproduce the system with minimal effort.

While these deliverables demonstrate the core concept of real-time drowsiness detection and alerting, several potential enhancements fall outside the current project scope. The prototype will not be mounted in a real car or connected to a vehicle’s onboard wiring; all demonstrations will occur under controlled, static conditions. There will be no road testing, custom mounting assembly development, or integration with other sensors, such as steering-angle encoders or seat pressure sensors. By limiting the scope to the core features of a drowsiness detection and alerting prototype, the project remains manageable. An agile approach will be employed to plan, develop, test, and iterate in short sprints, aiming to deliver a fully functioning, replicable prototype by the end of the year.


1.5.2 Limitation(s) in the Project
Despite system design considerations, several real-life constraints may limit the applicability and performance of Vigi-Drive. Real-world lighting on Kenyan roads is not constant; it varies widely from the bright African light of the afternoon to the dark, poorly lit streets at nighttime. The prototype requires a cheap webcam and basic facial landmark detection using the webcam and other libraries. Factors such as glare, shadowing, or head movement could affect the software's ability to identify the users' eyes or mouth, potentially missing an alert or producing false positives. While the software will be tested under both low-light and daylight conditions, it may not perform well enough under extreme conditions, such as direct bright sunlight or near pitch-black darkness, without an additional source of illumination or infrared light.

The rule-based method of classifying drowsiness using fixed thresholds for eye-aspect and mouth-aspect ratios could create a "one-size-fits-all" situation. There are significant individual differences in facial structure (e.g., eye shape, eyeglasses, facial hair) that could impact the software's accuracy of detection. Although conditions can be calibrated for a small number of test subjects, fixed threshold values cannot be assumed to be sufficiently adaptable or generalisable to a broader population without further data collection and the use of adaptive algorithms. Interaction between Python and the Arduino microcontroller through serial communication may introduce timing lag and require appropriate synchronisation for integration, which could hinder the transition of the prototype into a finished product.

Vigi-Drive’s controlled, stationary demonstrations cannot fully replicate the dynamics of a moving vehicle. Road vibrations, varying camera mounting angles, and the mental state of a driver in an actual driving environment could all influence system behaviour in unpredictable ways. Due to time and safety constraints, on-road trials fall outside the project’s scope, but they represent an essential step for future validation before any field deployment.


1.5.3 Delimitations in the Project
The evaluation will focus on a small, defined group of volunteer drivers, primarily university students and staff aged 18 to 35, who will use the Vigi-Drive prototype in a parked-car setting on campus or in a controlled parking lot. All testing and calibration will take place over the two semesters of the academic year, with each driver completing no more than three 20-minute sessions under both simulated “daylight” (well-lit indoors) and “low-light” (dimmed interior lighting) conditions. By restricting the study population to familiar faces and limiting the duration of each session, I can gather consistent feedback on how the facial landmark thresholds perform without overtaxing participants or extending beyond my project timeline.

Conversely, several factors lie outside this project’s remit to keep the research feasible and focused. I will not recruit professional drivers, older adults, or individuals with significant facial accessories (e.g., heavy makeup respirators) whose features might require separate calibration. No tests will be conducted in actual moving vehicles, on public roads, or at night on unlit streets since safety and logistical constraints prevent on-road trials. 



















Chapter Two: Literature Review
Introduction
This chapter reviews existing solutions for driver drowsiness detection, beginning with the behavioural science of fatigue and surveying current hardware- and software-based alert systems. It then examines various computer-vision approaches and evaluates their performance, cost, and accessibility. Special attention is paid to solutions designed for private vehicles in developing countries, highlighting gaps in affordability and ease of deployment.

Challenges Faced By Drivers In Kenya
Prolonged driving in Kenya’s rapidly growing private vehicle sector has exacerbated the risk of fatigue, yet robust detection tools remain scarce. A study of long-distance commercial drivers in Kenya found that diminished attention significantly contributed to accident rates, underscoring the real-world impact of unrecognized drowsiness (Nderu, 2022). Globally, driver fatigue is implicated in 10–30% of fatal crashes, with drowsy motorists facing up to a 2.5-fold increase in collision risk (Delwar et al., 2025). Despite these alarming figures, existing countermeasures in Kenya remain limited to public awareness campaigns and sporadic enforcement at rest stops rather than real-time monitoring. 

Technical obstacles further impede effective detection. High-performance deep learning models, such as recent transformer-based frameworks, that achieve exceptional eye-state classification accuracy (~98%) require extensive labelled datasets and powerful GPUs, making on-device, real-time inference challenging in resource-constrained settings. Computer–vision–only approaches suffer under varied lighting and occlusions; under direct sunlight or at night, facial-landmark trackers often lose reliability, leading to missed detections or false alarms.

 Single-modal systems overlook the multifaceted nature of fatigue. Hybrid methods that combine visual and physiological cues, such as those leveraging heart-rate variability alongside facial expressions, demonstrate improved robustness but at the cost of requiring intrusive sensor attachments or complex calibration.

Most solutions lack an integrated hardware alert that is both immediate and conspicuous. Smartphone apps may flash warnings or play sounds, but in noisy or hands-free driving contexts, such alerts can often go unnoticed (Hassan et al., 2025). Physical feedback, such as vibration motors or buzzers, has been demonstrated in research prototypes but remains uncommon in consumer products. These combined challenges of data and computational demands, environmental fragility, sensor intrusiveness, and insufficient alert delivery highlight the need for a low-cost, camera-based detection pipeline paired with a simple Arduino-driven buzzer, as proposed in VigiDrive, to reliably bridge the gap between research advances and real-world applicability. 

2.3. Related Applications / Solutions

Several software and research prototype solutions have been developed to detect driver drowsiness in real time. These systems vary in their reliance on mobile platforms, dedicated hardware, or hybrid approaches. However, each seeks to identify fatigue cues and alert the driver before a lapse leads to an accident.

2.3.1 Beep: Drowsiness Detection

“Beep: Drowsiness Detection” is a commercially available smartphone application that exemplifies a fully software-based solution. Designed for long drives and night commutes, Beep uses the phone’s front-facing camera to continuously analyse facial video for signs of sleepiness, primarily prolonged eye closure and yawning. When fatigue indicators cross a user-calibrated threshold, the app issues real-time audio alerts through paired AirPods and flashes the screen to regain the driver’s attention. Customisable sensitivity settings and claims of low-power background operation aim to strike a balance between detection accuracy and practical usability. However, Beep’s dependency on specific Bluetooth accessories and continuous smartphone operation constrains its applicability in vehicles without stable device mounts or for drivers who prefer not to use earphones.

2.3.2. Driver Drowsiness Alert Cam
Available on Android, Driver Drowsiness Alert Cam employs the phone camera to track facial landmarks and calculate an Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR). For sustained low-EAR events, the app emits a loud buzzer sound and displays an on-screen warning. It also logs drowsiness events with timestamps for post‐drive review (“Driver Drowsiness Alert Cam,” 2024). While this app adds a simple buzzer alert, it still relies on a single device for both detection and alarm, which may limit the alert's salience in loud cabins.


	 

2.3.3. Rajkumarsingha & Totah Drowsiness Detection
Available on Android, the Rajkumarsingha & Totah Drowsiness Detection app combines on-screen facial analysis with periodic fingertip heart rate sampling. It continuously captures the driver’s face via the front camera. It computes two behavioural metrics: Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR) to detect prolonged eye closure and Mouth Aspect Ratio (MAR) to identify yawning, using the Mobile Vision Face API.

Every ten minutes, the app prompts the driver to place a fingertip over the rear camera for a five-second photoplethysmographic (PPG) reading. From the captured intensity variations, it derives heart-rate variability (HRV) metrics, specifically RMSSD, to quantify autonomic arousal changes associated with fatigue.

A logistic regression fusion model then combines the real-time EAR/MAR indicators (updated every second) with the HRV score (updated with every cycle) to compute a unified drowsiness probability. When this probability exceeds a user-adjustable threshold, the app emits a loud buzzer from the phone’s speaker, flashes the display, and logs each event with a timestamp for later review.

While this hybrid approach achieves 93% accuracy in mixed on-road and simulator trials, it remains constrained by its reliance on a single device. Camera occlusion (e.g., sunglasses or poor lighting) can degrade facial detection, and the periodic fingertip test interrupts the driver’s focus. Moreover, speaker-only alerts may be missed in noisy environments, such as cabins.


2.4. Gaps in the Existing Applications/ Solutions
Despite the diversity of approaches embodied by Beep, Driver Drowsiness Alert Cam, and the Rajkumarsingha & Totah hybrid app, several common limitations persist. 

Single-device dependency leaves all three solutions vulnerable: they require an unobstructed camera view, which can be undermined by sunglasses, low-light conditions, or shifting head poses, and depend on the phone’s speaker for alerts, which may be inaudible in noisy cabins. Alert delivery remains purely auditory or visual, offering no tactile or multimodal warnings; in both Beep and the hybrid app, drivers must rely on sound and screen flashes that can go unnoticed if the phone is not in hand or if the interior environment is too bright or loud.

Environmental robustness is insufficient: camera-only detection often fails under direct sunlight or in dark nighttime driving, forcing users to compromise on detection performance or abandon the system altogether. The hybrid method’s periodic physiological test interrupts driving focus every ten minutes, potentially introducing its safety hazard as drivers divert attention to perform the fingertip measurement. Personalisation and calibration remain rudimentary: although sensitivity sliders are offered, none of the apps conduct an initial calibration to each driver’s baseline blink rate, yawning frequency, or HRV profile, leading to high false alarm rates or missed events for atypical users.

These gaps underscore the need for an integrated, multi-sensor solution that combines unobtrusive vision, physiological, and vehicular data streams; delivers multimodal alerts (auditory, visual, and haptic); and performs on-device, personalised calibration to protect all drivers reliably in real-world conditions.

Chapter Three: Development Methodology
Introduction
This chapter will describe the methodological framework that will guide the development of the VigiDrive Driver Drowsiness Detection System. It will explain how an Agile, iterative research approach structures each phase of the project, from gathering requirements through system design, implementation, and testing. The chapter will also introduce the main tools and technologies to be used: Python for video capture and processing; OpenCV and Dlib (or Mediapipe) for facial landmark detection; an Arduino board and a passive buzzer for hardware alerts; and PySerial for software-hardware communication. Finally, the expected deliverables, such as the facial landmark detection algorithm, the serial communication protocol, and the fully integrated prototype, will be outlined.

Software Development Methodology
The development of VigiDrive will adopt an Iterative and Incremental (Agile) methodology, enabling continuous refinement of both software and hardware components through a series of two-week sprints. Each sprint will deliver a specific functional increment, such as improved landmark detection, refined drowsiness thresholds, or more reliable alert signalling, and will incorporate feedback from simulated driving trials. This iterative process will ensure that emerging challenges, such as false alarms or performance bottlenecks, are addressed promptly and that user experiences inform subsequent design decisions.

Requirements Elicitation
Requirements for the VigiDrive system will be defined through three parallel activities. First, a comprehensive literature review of academic papers and industry reports on driver drowsiness detection will establish best practices and known challenges. Second, interviews and surveys will be conducted with private car drivers in Nairobi and along Kenya’s major highways to capture real-world fatigue patterns and user expectations. Third, consultations with road safety experts and representatives from the National Transport and Safety Authority (NTSA) will refine both functional and non-functional requirements, ensuring that the system meets local regulatory and operational constraints. The resulting requirements document will specify capabilities such as real-time detection of eye closure and yawning immediate buzzer alerts, reliable performance under variable lighting conditions, low latency, and cost constraints suitable for a student-built prototype.

Ideation and Planning Phase
In the ideation and planning phase, the project team will brainstorm potential technical solutions and evaluate them against project constraints, including budget, timeline, and available expertise. The system will be modularised into four primary components: video capture and preprocessing, facial-landmark detection and feature computation, fatigue classification and threshold logic, and hardware alert integration. A detailed Gantt chart will be developed to track key milestones, including the completion of the initial detection prototype by Week 4, the integration of Python-Arduino communication by Month 3, and the demonstration of the entire system by the end of Month 6.

Design Phase
During the design phase, system architecture diagrams and user-experience flows will be produced to define module interactions and data flows. The architecture will prioritise modularity and real-time performance: a VideoCaptureManager component will feed live frames into a Landmark Processor, which computes the Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR) and Mouth Aspect Ratio (MAR). A Fatigue Detector module will classify drowsiness events based on these metrics, triggering an Alert Controller that communicates with the Arduino board. Flowcharts and pseudocode will capture the detailed logic, ensuring that each module’s responsibilities are clearly defined and that the system can meet responsiveness targets.

Tool Selection and Setup
The selection of tools will prioritise open-source platforms that are widely supported and well-maintained. Python will be the primary development language, utilising OpenCV, Dlib, or Mediapipe for facial landmark detection and image processing. An Arduino Uno (or Nano) will be employed to control a passive buzzer for alert notifications, with PySerial handling the serial communication between the Python application and the Arduino. Development and testing will be conducted in Jupyter Notebook and Visual Studio Code, utilising Git for version control. All hardware components, including the webcam, jumper wires, and buzzer, will be procured from local suppliers to ensure cost-effectiveness.

Implementation Planning
Implementation will be organised into four sequential phases. Phase 1 will establish the core facial-landmark detection and calculate EAR and MAR from live webcam input. In Phase 2, the fatigue classification logic will be developed and validated using labelled video samples. Phase 3 will integrate the classification output with the Arduino sketch, ensuring that detected drowsiness events trigger the buzzer without undue delay. Phase 4 will integrate all modules into a single pipeline and conduct real-time testing in a simulated driving environment, allowing for threshold tuning and performance optimisation.

Coding and Development
During coding sprints, the development team will prototype the facial landmark module using Dlib’s 68-point predictor or Mediapipe’s Face Mesh API, capturing EAR and MAR values for each video frame. The fatigue-detection algorithm will then be implemented in Python, with events logged for later analysis. Concurrently, an Arduino sketch will be written to listen for serial commands and activate the buzzer. The integration will be achieved using PySerial, with a focus on minimising communication latency and ensuring reliable message delivery.

Testing and Deployment
Testing will be conducted at various levels. Unit tests will verify the precision of landmark extraction and ratio calculations. Integration tests will ensure end-to-end functionality, confirming that video capture, detection, and alerting systems operate in harmony. Performance testing will evaluate detection accuracy, frame-rate throughput, and buzzer response time, aiming for a latency of under 0.5 seconds. Simulated driving trials utilising pre-recorded highway footage or live webcam feeds in a stationary setup will offer practical insights for refining thresholds. Upon the successful completion of these tests, the final prototype and comprehensive documentation will be prepared for presentation in an academic setting.

3.4 Tools and Techniques
3.4.1 Python 3
Python 3 will serve as the primary development language for VigiDrive. Its straightforward syntax and extensive standard library enable rapid prototyping of both the video-processing pipeline and the control logic. Python’s ecosystem of scientific and hardware communication libraries will simplify tasks such as real‐time video capture, mathematical computation for fatigue metrics, and serial communication with Arduino.

3.4.3 OpenCV
OpenCV (Open-Source Computer Vision Library) will be used to interface with the webcam and pre‐process each video frame. Operations such as face detection, image conversion, and filtering are highly optimised in OpenCV’s C++ core and exposed via Python bindings, ensuring that the system can maintain a frame rate of at least 15–20 FPS on a typical student laptop.

3.4.4 Mediapipe / Dlib
Mediapipe / Dlib: Facial‐landmark detection will rely on either Google’s Mediapipe Face Mesh or Dlib’s 68‐point predictor. These libraries will pinpoint key facial features, specifically the eyes and mouth, on each frame, enabling the computation of the Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR) and Mouth Aspect Ratio (MAR). Mediapipe is favoured for its high CPU performance and easy installation, while Dlib is chosen for its proven accuracy in academic literature.

3.5.3 Drowsiness Classification Module 
This core software component will capture live video frames from the webcam and utilise Mediapipe or Dlib to identify key facial landmarks around the eyes and mouth. It will expose functions to compute the Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR) and Mouth Aspect Ratio (MAR) on every frame. The module will include confidence checks to handle partial occlusions or rapid head movements and will pass only valid ratio measurements to the next stage.

3.5.4 Alert and Logging Module 
Upon receiving a drowsiness flag, this module will perform two actions in parallel. First, it will send a serial command to the Arduino board to activate the buzzer, producing an immediate audible warning. Second, it will write a Drowsiness Event record to the MySQL database (or local CSV log) containing the timestamp, EAR, MAR, and session ID. A simple dashboard view within the application will allow the operator to see a live log of events and summary statistics (number of alerts, average response time) for that session.

4.2 Software Requirements Analysis
4.2.1 Functional Requirements
Functional requirements define the essential services the Vigi-Drive system must provide to ensure its successful operation:

User Authentication:
Users must be able to register and log in to the system for personalized monitoring. First-time users will be required to create an account, while returning users can log in to view previous session data. Authentication is managed through Firebase to ensure secure and seamless user identification.

Real-Time Drowsiness Detection:
The system shall continuously monitor the driver’s face using the webcam, processing each frame to detect signs of drowsiness based on eye and mouth aspect ratios (EAR and MAR). If drowsiness is detected for a sustained period, the system will trigger an alert. The Python application utilizes Mediapipe or OpenCV for facial landmark detection, calculating EAR and MAR to detect eye closure and yawning.

Alert System:
Upon detecting drowsiness, the system will activate an Arduino-controlled buzzer to alert the driver immediately. This alert is meant to prevent micro-sleep episodes and ensure timely intervention. The Python application communicates with the Arduino board via PySerial, sending a command to trigger the buzzer in real time.

Session Logging:
All detection events (time of detection, EAR/MAR values, and system alerts) will be logged for future review and analysis. Logs will be stored locally in CSV format, enabling easy export and analysis.

Calibration and Configuration:
Users can calibrate the system to their unique facial features, adjusting thresholds for eye and mouth aspect ratios based on individual characteristics (e.g., glasses, facial hair).A calibration screen will allow users to record baseline values, ensuring optimal detection accuracy under varying conditions.

4.2.2 Non-Functional Requirements
Non-functional requirements ensure the system performs well under real-world conditions, meeting performance, usability, and security standards:

Performance:
The system must operate in real time with minimal latency to ensure alerts are triggered promptly. Performance Metrics: The maximum allowed latency from detection to alert should not exceed 0.5 seconds.

Security:
User data must be securely handled, ensuring that no raw video data is stored. Only event logs and feature values are retained. Authentication is handled by Firebase for secure access, and sensitive data (e.g., facial landmarks) are processed without saving raw images.

Compatibility:
The system should work seamlessly on commonly used laptop operating systems (Windows, macOS, Linux) and maintain compatibility across various webcam models. The system is built using Python, with cross-platform libraries to ensure compatibility.

Accuracy and Reliability:
The system should demonstrate at least 90% accuracy in detecting driver drowsiness during controlled tests. The system will undergo repeated testing under varied lighting conditions to ensure its reliability.

Scalability:
The system should support multiple sessions per user and maintain consistent performance as the number of sessions grows. Data logs are designed with scalable structures to ensure easy integration into more comprehensive vehicle monitoring systems.

4.3 System Narrative
A typical interaction begins when the driver launches the Vigi-Drive application and selects the start command on the monitoring screen. The system immediately initialises the webcam, verifies camera availability, and renders a live preview to reassure the user that the face is in frame. For each captured frame, the FaceMesh component detects facial landmarks and the feature module derives eye aspect ratio and mouth aspect ratio. The inference engine transforms the features with a standard scaler and computes the probability of drowsiness using the trained classifier. A smoothing window accumulates consecutive positive predictions in order to suppress spurious alerts resulting from brief blinks or landmark jitter. When the probability remains above the threshold long enough to satisfy the window condition, the system declares a drowsiness event, raises a red visual banner on the preview, and, if configured, transmits a serial command to the Arduino which in turn actuates the buzzer. Every frame contributes summary statistics to the current session and every alert results in a durable log entry containing the timestamp, feature values, and classification confidence. The driver may stop monitoring at any time, after which the session is closed and appears in the history view where events can be inspected and exported for reporting.

5.4 Description of Model Training and Evaluation
This section outlines the complete process followed to train, optimize, and evaluate the drowsiness detection model integrated into the Vigi-Drive system. The training procedure began with preprocessing the Kaggle “Driver Fatigue Detection” dataset, which contains 9,120 labelled images across alert and fatigued conditions. Each image was processed using MediaPipe FaceMesh to extract 468 facial landmarks, from which the Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR) and Mouth Aspect Ratio (MAR) were derived. These numerical features formed the input dataset used to train the classification model. The dataset was partitioned into training (70%), validation (20%), and testing (10%) subsets to ensure that the model’s performance could be assessed fairly and consistently. The model was trained to recognise patterns in EAR/MAR values that correspond to drowsy behaviour, and was later evaluated using standard machine learning metrics to validate accuracy, robustness, and suitability for real-time deployment.


Figure 12: Sreenshot shwoing processing
5.4.1 Logistic Regression Model Components

The Vigi-Drive system incorporates a Logistic Regression classifier as its primary model for drowsiness prediction. Logistic Regression was selected due to its efficiency, interpretability, and strong performance in binary decision-making tasks. The model receives two core inputs—EAR and MAR—which quantify eye closure levels and mouth opening levels respectively. Before training, these features were normalised using a MinMax scaler to reduce the impact of value magnitude differences and to stabilise the optimisation process. During training, the model learns optimal parameter weights that best separate the alert state from the drowsy state. The model outputs a probability value p(drowsy) for each frame, representing the likelihood that the driver is fatigued. This probability is compared against a configurable decision threshold, and when exceeded for a defined number of consecutive frames, the system triggers the alert pipeline. The lightweight nature of the classifier makes it ideal for continuous, real-time inference without compromising speed or computational efficiency.
5.4.2 Model Evaluation Metrics
To assess the reliability and overall performance of the trained classifier, several evaluation metrics were computed using the test dataset. Accuracy was used to determine the proportion of total predictions that the model classified correctly. Precision measured how many of the frames labelled as drowsy were truly drowsy, ensuring that false alarms were minimized. Recall quantified the model’s ability to correctly identify all actual drowsy instances, which is critical in safety-related applications. The F1-score, defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, provided a balanced indicator of performance by accounting for both false positives and false negatives. Collectively, these metrics demonstrated that the classifier was sufficiently accurate and dependable for real-time driver monitoring, supporting its integration into the Vigi-Drive system for operational use.

5.5 System Implementation
The implemented system follows the modular architecture described in the previous chapter. The modules are integrated through a controller that manages the lifecycle of a monitoring session, requests services from individual components, and exposes state for the user interface to render. The following paragraphs describe the principal modules that comprise the delivered prototype and summarise how each was tested.

5.5.1 Capture and Landmark Module
The capture module interfaces with the webcam and delivers frames at the configured resolution and frame rate. It is responsible for verifying camera availability, handling device selection, and exposing a clean shutdown when monitoring stops. Each frame is forwarded to the landmark submodule which wraps the MediaPipe FaceMesh detector with parameters chosen for reliability over speed. Functional tests confirmed that the module starts and stops deterministically, that frames are delivered without resource leaks during hour-long sessions, and that landmarks are detected for a range of head poses and lighting conditions typical of indoor environments.
5.5.2 Feature and Inference Module
The feature module computes the eye aspect ratio and mouth aspect ratio from the returned landmarks and then produces the four-element feature vector in the exact order used during model training. The inference module loads the serialized scaler and classifier and returns a calibrated probability of drowsiness for every frame. A light-weight temporal decision component maintains a counter of consecutive frames above threshold and only signals a positive event when the counter exceeds the configured window length. Unit tests verified numerical consistency between offline feature extraction and the runtime implementation, while integration tests confirmed that probabilities remained stable under constant scenes and that alerts were suppressed for single-frame anomalies.

5.5.3 Alerting and Serial Module
The alerting module renders the primary visual warning by drawing a red banner across the top of the preview window with an unambiguous message. When serial alerting is enabled, the serial submodule opens the configured port and transmits a short command understood by the Arduino whenever the decision layer confirms drowsiness. Bench tests with a connected buzzer demonstrated that the round-trip delay from threshold crossing to audible output remained comfortably below one second, which satisfies the responsiveness requirement for an in-cabin alerting system. The module also handles error conditions, such as a missing port, by logging the incident and continuing operation without crashing the session.

5.5.4 Logging and History Module
The logging module writes a row to the events file whenever an alert is triggered and attaches the timestamp, the feature values, and the model probability so that later analysis can be conducted without access to raw video. At the end of a session, a summary record is appended to a session log with start and end times and configuration settings. The history view consumes these files to populate a compact timeline for each session and provides an export action that produces a comma-separated file suitable for spreadsheets and statistical software. Tests verified that logs are created on first use, that concurrent events are appended without loss, and that exported files open correctly in common analysis tools.
